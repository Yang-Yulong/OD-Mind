# A B S T R A C T  

The composition and distribution of deep-sea sediments is the result of a multitude of climatic, biotic and oceanic conditions relating to biogeochemical cycles and environmental change. Here we utilize the extensive sediment archives of the International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP) and its predecessors to construct maps of deep-sea sediment type across two critical but contrasting boundaries in the Paleogene, one characterised by an interval of extreme warmth (Paleocene/Eocene) and the other by global cooling (Eocene/Oligocene). Ocean sediment distribution shows significant divergence both between the latest Paleocene and Paleocene Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM), across the Eocene-Oligocene Transition (EOT), and in comparison to modern se­ diment distributions. Carbonate sedimentation in the latest Paleocene extends to high southern latitudes. Disappearance of carbonate sediments at the PETM is well documented and can be attributed to dissolution caused by significant ocean acidification as a result of carbon-cycle perturbation. Biosiliceous sediments are rare and it is posited that the reduced biogenic silica deposition at the equator is commensurate with an overall lack of equatorial upwelling in the early Paleogene ocean. In the Southern Ocean, we attribute the low in biosiliceous burial, to the warm deep water temperatures which would have impacted biogenic silica preservation. In the late Eocene, our sediment depositional maps record a tongue of radiolarian ooze in the eastern equatorial Pacific. Enhanced biosiliceous deposits in the late Eocene equatorial Pacific and South Atlantic are due to increased productivity and the spin-up of the oceans. Our compilation documents the enhanced global carbonate sedi­ mentation in the early Oligocene, confirming that the drop in the carbonate compensation depth was global.  

# 1. Introduction  

Deep-sea sediments represent the most widespread and complete record of Cenozoic oceanic and climatic state. The pattern and com­ position of seafloor sediments seaward of the continental shelf is a re­ flection of biological, geochemical, terrestrial and climatic conditions on Earth at the time of deposition (Chester and Jickells, 2012). Sedi­ mentary changes through the geological record are an essential tool in reconstructing past conditions on Earth, constraining key changes in the atmosphere-ocean system and facilitating the characterization of sig­ nificant climatic perturbations. As such, the study of paleo-sediment records facilitates the examination of broad scale oceanic and climatic changes including but not limited to ocean productivity, carbonate  

chemistry, and glacial processes.  

The overall purpose of this study was to examine the distribution of seafloor sediments as the Earth transitioned from the warm conditions of the late Paleocene to the icehouse conditions of the early Oligocene. We created a compilation of all major deep sea sediments across two major climate transitions, the PETM and EOT. We aimed to determine how paleoceanographic phenomena were reflected in the sediment pattern of these two critical and contrasting climate intervals, and explore how and why the distribution in the Paleogene differs to the modern.  

Over the past half century, the International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP) and its predecessors have cored deep-sea sediments throughout the oceans, resulting in an ever growing archive of paleo­ climatological data. Here we collate and synthesize data from deep-sea archives from over 175 DSDP/ODP/IODP sites (up to IODP Expedition 362) and present a compilation of global ocean sediment distribution maps.  We  concentrate  on  four  time  intervals;  latest  Paleocene (\~57–56 Ma), PETM (\~55.9 Ma), latest Eocene (\~34 Ma) and earliest Oligocene (\~33.5 Ma). The tables provide a catalogue of all existing ocean drilling sites covering these intervals drilled to date. In mapping ocean sediment distribution for the upper Paleocene, PETM, upper Eocene and lower Oligocene, this study has produced a functional vi­ sual resource in understanding the biogeochemical change and oceanic response to climate perturbations. These maps provide a record of se­ dimentary changes on a global scale across major climate transitions to compare with the sediment distribution in the modern oceans, and contribute to the understanding of environmental change across two critical intervals in the Paleogene. We review the global implications of paleoclimate on ocean sedimentation and the impact of changes in ocean dissolution and productivity.  

# 1.1. Modern global distribution of marine sediments  

Knowledge of modern sediment distribution in the global ocean and its controls provide a better understanding of global biogeochemical cycles and response of marine sediment to environmental and climatic changes. Oceanography and marine geology textbooks generally con­ tain a map of the global oceanic sediment distribution (e.g., Davies and Gorsline, 1976; Barron and Whitman, 1981; Hüneke and Mulder, 2011), which normally illustrates five or six main types of sediments in the ocean basins based on their primary composition and provenance. Modern sediment distribution in the ocean is determined by the sedi­ ment sources, as well as the physical and chemical factors that control deposition.  

Marine sediments can be divided into two main categories, litho­ genous and biogenous (Lyle, 2014). Lithogenous sediments derive from weathering and erosional processes on land and underwater, including submarine volcanoes and consist of terrigenous sediments, glacial se­ diments and deep-sea/red clays. Most prominent are river-borne sedi­ ments that form voluminous deposits near land. Glaciomarine sedi­ ments include glacially transported sediments, such as ice-rafted debris. In the modern oceans, glaciomarine sediments occupy high latitude areas, especially the margins around Antarctica and in the North Atlantic (Fig. 1). Aeolian sediments/deep-sea clays generally accumu­ late below a depth of \~5000 m in the modern ocean (Chester and Jickells, 2012). They cover large areas of the ocean where sedimenta­ tion rates are low and where only windblown dust can accumulate. Volcanic ash can be locally important near island arcs.  

Biogenous sediments, often referred as oozes, are produced from biotic processes and composed of calcareous and siliceous microfossils. A primary control on biogenic sedimentation is ocean productivity which impacts the types and distribution of marine organisms, and the rate of fossil test production and deposition. Biogenic sediments are also subject to dissolution, which affects carbonate and silica preservation and further shapes the areal coverage of the deposit. Major climate changes can have a crucial impact on marine biodiversity and dis­ solution, resulting in different oceanic sediment patterns.  

Calcareous oozes are a major component of deep-sea sediments which originate from calcareous micro-organisms, mainly from plank­ tonic foraminifera and calcareous nannofossils. The distribution of calcareous sediments in the ocean is principally controlled by the depth of the lysocline and carbonate compensation depth (CCD), which re­ flects the chemistry of the ocean carbonate system (Chester and Jickells, 2012). Maximum dissolution occurs below the lysocline, and beneath the CCD sediments are carbonate free (Takahashi and Broecker, 1977). The CCD varies in each ocean basin; the modern CCD in the Pacific is \~3500–5000 m, and in the Atlantic and Indian ocean is 4500–5000 m (Berger et al., 1976; Chester and Jickells, 2012).  

Siliceous oozes are composed of siliceous skeletal remains derived mainly  from  diatoms  and  radiolarians.  In  the  modern  ocean, biosiliceous dominated sediments are mainly focused in a few distinct areas, particularly in the Southern Ocean (diatom oozes), and equa­ torial Pacific (mixed carbonate-opal biogenic sediments) (Fig. 1). The distribution of siliceous sediments is primarily controlled by pro­ ductivity and dissolution (Chester and Jickells, 2012; Ragueneau et al., 2000). A positive relationship has been shown between areas of high productivity and siliceous sediment accumulation (Calvert, 1974; Garcia et al., 2010; Barron and Baldauf, 1989). Aluminium concentra­ tions also impact the degree of biogenic silica preservation (DeMaster, 2014). Preservation of siliceous organisms in modern ocean sediments is very low (1–5%) compared to the production rate in surface waters, as the oceans are undersaturated in silica at all depths (Siever, 1957; Calvert, 1974; Ragueneau et al., 2000; Barron et al., 2015).  

Sediment deposition on the ocean floor is not a simple reflection of productivity in surface waters as it involves many factors (Hüneke and Henrich, 2011). Dutkiewicz et al. (2015) produced the first digital map of modern oceanic sediments showing that distribution of seafloor se­ diment lithology is more complicated than the earlier hand-drawn maps and biogenic sediments show a strong relationship with sea surface nutrients, salinity and temperature.  

# 1.2. Paleocene Eocene thermal Maximum  

The PETM (55.93–55.71 Ma; Westerhold et al., 2017) represents a short-lived  but  dramatic  hyperthermal  event,  characterised  by a  >  2‰ negative carbon isotope excursion in marine and terrestrial sections (Kennett and Stott, 1991; Koch et al., 1992; Dickens et al., 1995; Zachos et al., 2005; Dickens, 2011). Although the mechanism is still debated, the PETM is thought to have resulted from a multi-pulsed release of depleted carbon into Earth's exogenic carbon reservoir (Zeebe et al., 2009; Bowen et al., 2015; Frieling et al., 2016). The addition of thousands of petagrams of carbon (Meissener et al., 2014) to the oceanatmosphere system had a profound effect on physical, geochemical and biotic processes on Earth. Ocean temperatures increased significantly, with average SSTs increasing by \~4 °C or more (Dunkley Jones et al., 2013; Frieling et al., 2017) and deep ocean temperatures increasing by \~6 °C (Kennett and Stott, 1991). Surface ocean pH decreased by 0.3 pH units whilst the lysocline and CCD shoaled by > 2 km (Zachos et al., 2005; Penman et al., 2014; Bralower et al., 2018). Increased atmo­ spheric warming, against a late Paleocene world with atmospheric CO2 concentrations hundreds of ppmv greater than today and ice-free poles, resulted in a reduced latitudinal temperature gradient, weakening of the trade winds, significant alteration to the rate and nature of mer­ idional overturning circulation and an overall decrease in ocean ven­ tilation and nutrient availability (Winguth et al., 2012; Pälike et al., 2014; Heinze and Ilyina, 2015). On the seafloor benthic foraminifera suffered a major extinction, with 35–50% of species affected (Kennett and Stott, 1991; Thomas and Shackleton, 1996).  

# 1.3. Eocene-Oligocene transition  

The Eocene-Oligocene Transition (EOT; ca. 34–33.5 Ma) is one of the most significant episodes of climatic change in the Cenozoic. Through the Eocene the global climate developed from a warm, ice-free “greenhouse”  world  to  the  glacial  Antarctic  “icehouse”  of  the Oligocene. Benthic foraminiferal oxygen isotope records show a rapid positive excursion across the EOT (e.g., Kennett and Shackleton, 1976; Miller et al., 1991; Zachos et al., 1996; Coxall et al., 2005; Katz et al., 2008), reflecting the establishment of continental-scale Antarctic ice sheets. Increased ice volume is additionally indicated by ice-rafted debris in the Southern Ocean and a major drop in global sea level (Kennett and Shackleton, 1976; Miller et al., 1991, 2008a, 2008b; Zachos et al., 1996; Schulte et al., 2009). Across the EOT there was a rapid and permanent deepening of the CCD (van Andel and Moore, 1974; Heath et al., 1977; Lyle et al., 2002; Coxall et al., 2005; Pälike et al., 2012), terrestrial and marine cooling (Zanazzi et al., 2007; Wade et al., 2012), and biotic turnover (Prothero and Berggren, 1992; Dunkley Jones et al., 2008; Pearson et al. 2008; Wade and Pearson, 2008; Wade et al., 2018; Houben et al., 2019a). The causal mechanisms are a decrease in atmospheric CO2 below a critical threshold (Pearson et al., 2009), coupled with orbital forcing (Pälike et al., 2006).

# 2. Methodology  

# 2.1. Data compilation  

Sediment data (Tables 1-3) were sourced from the archives of the International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP) and its predecessors (Deep Sea Drilling Project, DSDP; Ocean Drilling Program, ODP; In­ tegrated Ocean Drilling Program, and International Ocean Discovery Program, IODP) representing over 50 years of ocean sediment core sampling and a significant repository of primary geological data. To identify DSDP/ODP/IODP sites with a sedimentological record covering the study intervals (late Paleocene / early Eocene and late Eocene / early Oligocene), a review of literature was performed, with shipboard data for sites referenced therein compiled. To identify the target intervals, we used all geochronological information available, including calcareous and si­ liceous biostratigraphy, magnetostratigraphy and stable isotope strati­ graphy, though there are very few sites where all information is available.  

The PETM was identified at sites either by the Shipboard Scientific Party or by referral to post cruise literature (e.g., Kelly et al., 2012; Hollis et al., 2015; Frieling et al., 2019; Penman et al., 2019; Witkowski et al., 2020a). To detect the PETM interval in cruise reports predating its recognition as a significant global climatic perturbation (chiefly DSDP/ODP sites cored prior to 1991), the Paleocene/Eocene boundary was identified through plankton biostratigraphy, and/or the benthic foraminiferal extinction. In a number of sites cored before 1991, sub­ sequent investigations have re-examined core samples to obtain δ13C values and established the position of the negative carbon isotope ex­ cursion (CIE). Such sites are also included in our dataset. For the late Paleocene, we targeted the interval from 57 to 56 Ma. At sites with a carbon isotope stratigraphy, we selected the interval prior to the CIE (\~56 Ma), providing that stratigraphic control indicated that sedi­ mentation was continuous.  

The DSDP, ODP and IODP sites that contain sedimentary records of the EOT are primarily identified based on the scientific literature. Only sites that contain complete sections spanning the Eocene-Oligocene boundary without hiatuses are used, as confirmed by age-depth models (e.g., Firth et al., 2013; Houben et al., 2019b; Witkowski et al., 2020a). Several ocean cores covering the Eocene-Oligocene boundary are in­ complete or condensed, for example DSDP sites 216, 217, 460, and ODP sites 689, 742, 752, 758 and 1172 (Houben et al., 2019b). Hiatuses across the EOT have been attributed to an increase in ocean circulation vigour and glacioeustatic sea-level fall associated with the climate shift (Kennett and Shackleton, 1976; Zachos et al., 1996; Miller et al., 2008a, 2008b, 2009; Houben et al., 2019a). However, it is possible that some hiatuses were not detected due to poor geochronological records. As the Eocene-Oligocene boundary was formally defined in 1993, the EoceneOligocene boundary in the ocean drilling site reports prior to 1993 is identified from the biostratigraphy (e.g., Wade et al., 2011). There are uncertainties about the boundary in some sites owing to lack of marker fossils. For the late Eocene, we targeted the calcareous nannofossil CP15/CP16 (NP19-20/NP21) zonal boundary (\~34 Ma), within Chron C13r. For the early Oligocene at sites with an oxygen isotope strati­ graphy, we selected the interval at the start of the early Oligocene glacial maximum (\~33.5 Ma), corresponding to Chron C13n, and low latitude radiolarian RP19/RP20 zonal boundary.  

Sections that are condensed were included in our data set (e.g., EOT at ODP Site 1217), but incomplete sections due to hiatuses, core gaps, significant drilling disturbance and poor recovery are excluded (e.g., PETM at DSDP sites 152, 215, 259, 313, 316, 464, 524, 528, 529, 530, 576, 577, 596, ODP sites 634, 698, 699, 738, 807, 869, 1050, 1183, 1217, 1257 and IODP Site U1407). Site 1051 is retained, although it should be noted that previous studies have suggested that the PETM at this site is incomplete (Farley and Eltgroth, 2003; Röhl et al., 2003; Nicolo et al., 2007). Site 327 was also retained in this study, though siliceous sedimentation ceased \~56.3 Ma (Witkowski et al., 2020a). These data from DSDP, ODP and IODP drilling sites were collected and organized using spreadsheets (Tables 2 and 3) in order to produce comprehensive ocean sediment maps. Our data set provides an in­ ventory of all ocean drilling sites covering these two critical boundaries. The ocean sediment distribution maps are based on 46 sites for the PETM and 175 sites for the EOT (Tables 2 and 3).  

# 2.2. Sediment classification  

To determine the dominant sediment type, the lithologies were examined using barrel sheets, visual core descriptions and smear slide data reported by shipboard scientists. These were compared and cor­ roborated to ensure correct identification of major sediment compo­ nents for each site across the PETM and EOT interval. The sediment classification in this study adopts a simplified version of the scheme proposed by Mazzullo et al. (1988), which is the standard classification used by the DSDP, ODP and IODP. Sediments are classified based on their major component as recorded in shipboard data archives. Where sediments are described with modifiers they are classified based on the major modifier. An example of this would be ‘nannofossil chalk’, wherein ‘nannofossil’ acts as the major modifier, indicating nanno­ fossils as the dominant sediment component, before the principle name, chalk, indicating calcareous composition and degree of consolidation (Mazzullo et al., 1988).

Five main types of sediments are classified and used in sedimentary maps in this study. The sediments are classified as calcareous, siliceous, clay, terrigenous (siliciclastic) and glaciomarine. Calcareous sediments include foraminiferal and nannofossil oozes/chalk/limestone. Siliceous sediments include radiolarian and diatom oozes, chert and porcellanite which represent dense siliceous sediments. Clay consists of sediment (< 2 μm), including claystone. Terrigenous sediments generally consist of sand, silt and mud. Diamictite represents the glaciomarine sediments. The details of sediment classification can be found in Tables 1-3.  

Where carbonate sediments are reported as indurated, such as chalk or limestone, these are mapped as oozes if a major biogenic sediment component is indicated. This is a reflection of the notion that lithifi­ cation occurred largely as a post-depositional process, and that when they were deposited, they would have been poorly consolidated cal­ careous sediments (oozes) (Chester and Jickells, 2012). Similarly, where the suffix ‘stone’ is used in shipboard rock descriptions to in­ dicate lithified siliclastic sediments (Mazzullo et al., 1988), this is dis­ regarded on the sediment maps and claystone becomes clay. Porcella­ nite and chert are derived from biogenic opal, and are considered to be the diagenetic transformation of biosiliceous oozes. However, we con­ tinued to distinguish radiolarian and diatom ooze from porcellanite and chert on the maps and tables, as the nature of the original biosiliceous deposition (diatom versus radiolarian ooze) is unknown.  

# 2.3. Mapping sediment distributions across critical Paleogene climate transitions  

The sediment distribution maps are produced based on sedimentary records from ocean drilling sites. To accurately reconstruct paleogeo­ graphies of tectonic plates and locations of drilling sites, GPlates (http://www.gplates.org; Boyden et al., 2011) open source plate reconstruction software was used in conjunction with a dynamic plate reconstruction model (Seton et al., 2012 and references therein). Ocean drilling sites were created as individual features in GPlates and posi­ tioned based on present-day longitude and latitude. Specific plate IDs were assigned in line with the defined topology in Seton et al. (2012). A reconstruction was then performed from 56 and 34 Ma to produce a base map onto which sediment distribution could be mapped. The ArcGIS software was used to create site labels and graticules, and the sedimentary maps were drawn using Adobe Illustrator CS4 vector based graphics software. In our paleogeographic maps for the late Eocene and early Oligocene, the Drake Passage is open.

# 2.4. Sediment extrapolation  

As well as mapping sediments based on recorded lithologies for individual drilling sites, some sediment types are extrapolated. The paleobathymetry of the ocean basin model from Muller et al. (2008) (http://www.earthbyte.org), early Paleogene reconstructions of paleo­ bathymetry (He et al., 2019), and the CCD records from literature are used to infer the calcareous depositional areas. This was achieved by comparison with reconstructed positions of mid-ocean ridges, as these by their nature represent areas of significant elevation above the sea­ floor. As the primary control of carbonate sediment distribution is de­ position above the CCD, sediments of this type could be extrapolated along mid ocean ridges with medium confidence for the late Paleocene. For the PETM sediment map, carbonate sediments are not extrapolated to reflect PETM dissolution. Conversely, as clays dominate sediments deposited below the CCD, clays are inferred as the major sediment type on abyssal plains.  

Carbonate sedimentation is extrapolated for the EOT based on a CCD depth in the late Eocene of approximately 3700 m in the Pacific Ocean (Pälike et al., 2012), with the magnitude of deepening across the EOT of more than 1 km (van Andel, 1975; Rea and Lyle, 2005; Pälike et al., 2012). Specifically, the Pacific CCD deepened to 5000 m in Pa­ cific equatorial regions (van Andel, 1975; Heath et al., 1977), in the Indian Ocean from \~3600 to 4000 m (Rea and Lyle, 2005), and in the South Atlantic, evidence from DSDP Legs 73 (Hsu et al., 1984) and 74 (Moore et al., 1984) indicates the CCD dropped from \~3300 m to 4300 m. Terrigenous (siliciclastic) sediments are extrapolated in the areas near the continents.  

A hindrance to complete characterization of sediment distribution arises from biases in the locations and distribution of ocean drilling sites, because paleoceanographic drilling expeditions preferentially target carbonate rich sediments. Large areas of the Southern Ocean, South Pacific, central North Pacific and Arctic Oceans remain virtually un-sampled. In addition, the paleobathymetry map for extrapolation in the Paleogene is of poor resolution. In the context of the limited data available however, this study was designed to present a picture of Paleogene sediment distribution. The pattern of sediment distribution in our maps is a simplified version based on primary controls and data availability, and inevitably under-represents the complexity of sedi­ ment deposition, as shown in the modern seafloor map of Dutkiewicz et al. (2015). Many locations show a different lithology to a nearby site, due to the scale of maps and heterogeneous ocean floor conditions (such as varying topography).  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Upper Paleocene sediment distribution  

On upper Paleocene sediment map (Fig. 2) carbonate sediments dominate the sites sampled, which are predominately on topographic highs, and clay is the principal sediment elsewhere. Of the 46 DSDP, ODP and IODP drilling sites for which the PETM was recovered, only six sites (sites 327, 959, 1171, 1208, M0004, U1403), have non‑carbonate dominated sediment immediately prior to the onset of the PETM. With the exception of the Arctic basin, all major ocean basins show evidence of regional carbonate deposition. The majority of carbonate sediments are dominated by nannofossil oozes, with generic calcareous oozes the second most abundant carbonate sediments, and a minor component of foraminiferal oozes recorded. Siliceous sediments are rare; radiolarian ooze is recorded only at Site 327, porcellanite at Site 959, and no diatomaceous oozes are present in the sampled data. In terms of southern hemisphere carbonate sedimentation, there appears to be no latitudinal constraints on sediment deposition, with nannofossil oozes recorded as far south as Site 690 in the Weddell Sea, although, Site 1172 on the East Tasman rise, is dominated by clayey silt. The only ocean basin lacking in carbonate sedimentation in the late Paleocene is the Arctic, though with only one site sampled (Site M0004), it is diffi­ cult to conclusively say whether the clay sediment at this site represents a regional feature or an anomalous data point. Site U1403, located in the central North Atlantic Ocean, is also dominated by clay across the interval of study. In the western Pacific Ocean Site 1208, located near the modern-day Shatsky rise, displayed a dominant sediment fraction of clay just prior to the PETM. Other western Pacific sites located near the modern-day Shatsky rise (Sites 305, 1209, 1210, 1211 and 1212) are nannofossil ooze dominated in the late Paleocene. Sites 1215, 1220 and 1221 in the equatorial eastern Pacific are all carbonate dominated, as are the sediments at sites 1001 and 999 in the Caribbean Sea. Seafloor sediments at sites 929, 1258, 1259, 1260 in the equatorial to tropical Atlantic Ocean are chiefly nannofossil oozes in the late Paleocene. In the north east Atlantic Ocean, sites 549 and 550, on the western edge of the Goban Spur, are dominated by nannofossil ooze; whilst Site 401, located in the Bay of Biscay in the early Paleogene, had a sedimentary cover of calcareous ooze in the late Paleocene. Carbonate deposits were added to the maps across parts of the peri-Tethys region for the late Paleocene (Ouda et al., 2016; Luciani et al., 2007; Alegret et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2013; Heinze and Ilyina, 2015).  

# 3.2. PETM sediment distribution  

The PETM map (Fig. 3) represents a major shift in sediment com­ ponent from upper Paleocene nannofossil ooze to PETM clay. This is clearly shown in southern Atlantic sites 525, 527, 1262, 1263, 1265, 1266 and 1267. Sites 689, 690 and 700 in the South Atlantic and Site 605 in the North Atlantic however, remain nannofossil ooze. Siliceous sedimentation (radiolarian ooze) ceased at \~56.3 Ma at Site 327 (Witkowski et al., 2020a) and was overlain by clay. In the tropical to equatorial Atlantic Ocean, sites 929, 1258, 1259 and 1260 also record a transition from nannofossil oozes in the late Paleocene to clay and, in the Caribbean Sea, sites 999 and 1001 record diminished carbonate deposition and a switch to clay as the dominant sediment fraction. Equatorial Pacific sites 1215, 1220 and 1221 record a comparable re­ duction in carbonate deposition, with clays deposited during the PETM. However, at Site 865, to the northwest of sites 1220 and 1221, de­ position of foraminiferal oozes persisted across the PETM, and at sites 305, 1210, 1211 and 1212, a change in sediment type is not recorded, with these sites remaining nannofossil ooze-dominated. Shatsky Rise (Site 1209) changes from nannofossil ooze-dominated to clay-domi­ nated, whilst the predominance of clays as the major lithology at Site 1208 persisted across the PETM. North Atlantic Site 1051 consists of siliceous carbonate ooze in both the upper Paleocene and PETM, and in the northeast Atlantic Ocean, sediments at sites 401, 549, 550 and U1409 consist predominately of clay throughout this interval; Site U1403, like Site 1208, remains clay-dominated. At site M0004 in the Arctic Basin, clays persisted as the major sediment component across the PETM. The southern Indian Ocean and proto-Southern Ocean sites 750, 752, 762 and 1135 appear to be least affected in terms of carbo­ nate dissolution, and all remained nannofossil ooze-dominated across the PETM, though nearby Site 747 shifted from nannofossil ooze to clay. Site 1172 on the East Tasman rise remained silt-dominated across the PETM, whilst Site 277 in the southwestern Pacific records majority carbonate deposition at the PETM. High southern latitude sites 702 and 748 transition from chalk to chert across the PETM, and Site 959, in the east Atlantic remains porcellanite.  

# 3.3. Upper Eocene sediment distribution  

The ocean sediment core data indicate that the sediments deposited in the late Eocene were dominantly calcareous (Table 3). Approxi­ mately 100 sites show carbonate sediments distributed in all major ocean basins (Fig. 4), and a high concentration of calcareous-dominated sediment sites are located in the South Atlantic, Indian Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico. However, the calcareous sediments are not restricted to the low-latitude areas; sites in the Southern Ocean and North Atlantic also have carbonate sediments in the upper Eocene.  

Terrigenous (siliceous) sediments were recovered from \~15 late Eocene sites and consist mainly of radiolarian ooze with some diatom ooze. Radiolarian oozes are highly concentrated in the eastern equa­ torial Pacific (Fig. 5); only one site (Site 291) with radiolarian ooze is located in the western equatorial Pacific (Fig. 4). Sites 511 and 1090, situated in southern part of the South Atlantic Ocean are diatom oozes. Porcellanite is recorded at Site 959, located in the eastern equatorial Atlantic.  

Siliciclastic sediments are generally found in sites that were situated near the continents, however, they are also found in the ocean basins. Turbidite-derived silty sand and clayey silt are recorded at Site 386, and volcanic sand at Site 841 (Fig. 4).  

Clay was present in all the major oceans (Fig. 4), but mainly con­ centrated in the North Pacific and South Pacific oligotrophic gyres. Site 913, on the present day East Greenland margin, is the northernmost location in the EOT sediment distribution map and records clay sedi­ ments from the upper Eocene. Site 739, near Antarctica, has diatomite sediments of late Eocene age, but nearby, Site 1166 has only claydominated sediments.  

# 3.4. Lower Oligocene sediment distribution  

The lower Oligocene map (Fig. 6) shows a significant change from upper Eocene non-calcareous sediments including siliceous oozes, clay and terrigenous/siliciclastic sediment, to lower Oligocene calcareous sediments. The majority of late Eocene sites accumulating radiolarian sediments shifted to calcareous oozes in the early Oligocene. In the equatorial Pacific, most sites containing siliceous sediment transition from radiolarian-dominated sediment to nannofossil oozes as the major sediment component in the early Oligocene (Fig. 7). Only Sites 163 and 166 continued to see deposition of radiolarian dominated sediments. Apart from the significant change of radiolarian dominated sediments, some clay dominated sediments and terrigenous/siliciclastic sediments also changed to calcareous sediments through the EOT. Site 77 in the Pacific Ocean, Site 224 in Arabian sea and eastern Atlantic Ocean Site 547 showed a major component shift from clay to calcareous deposition at this time interval. Sites 1170 and 1171 in the Southern Ocean also record the change from terrigenous/siliciclastic to carbonate sediments during the EOT. Sites that recorded calcareous sedimentation during the late Eocene continued to see calcareous sedimentation across the EOT. The diatom oozes at Site 1090 and Site 511 in the South Atlantic Ocean and porcellanite at Site 959 in the eastern equatorial Atlantic persisted across the transition.  

# 4. Discussion  

# 4.1. Sediment distribution in the late Paleocene, and comparison to modern  

Whilst it is well established that shoaling of the CCD occurred, our compilation indicates the extent of carbonate distribution before and during the PETM. Comparative analysis of the maps produced herein (Figs. 2 and 3), both to one another and to those showing modern ocean sediment distributions (Fig. 1), demonstrates how key oceanic and climatic parameters are altered by this perturbation in Earth's climate state.  


Upper Paleocene deep-sea sediments are dominated by carbonates and clay. The distribution of carbonate sediments for the upper Paleocene (Fig. 2) shows marked differences compared to that of the modern, particularly in the Southern Ocean. In the modern Southern Ocean, diatomaceous oozes represent the dominant sediment type (Fig. 1), with only minor and isolated regions of carbonate sediment deposition (Dutkiewicz et al., 2015). Conversely, the upper Paleocene sediment map (Fig. 2) shows broad regions of carbonate ooze around the Antarctic continent, reflecting differences in the late Paleocene ocean state compared to the modern. A primary control on carbonate sediment distribution in the open ocean is deposition above the CCD (van Andel, 1975). Paleobathymetry data for sites recording carbonate sediment deposition in the upper Paleocene show that all are located on relief features on the seafloor or along shallow continental margins. The broad region of nannofossil ooze to the west of Australia is inferred from sites located on-top or on-flank of the Kerguelen Plateau, and would have been significantly shallower than the surrounding seafloor (Frey et al., 2000). Similarly, the remainder of upper Paleocene car­ bonate sites sampled sediments deposited on submarine rises, ridges, plateaus, guyots and continental margins. The paucity of upper Paleo­ cene siliceous sediments in the deep-sea realm has been taken to signify the confinement of biogenic siliceous sedimentation to the shelves, especially considering the extensive distribution of late Paleocene through early Eocene diatomite on the Eurasian Platform. On the other hand, however, the apparent scarcity of siliceous sediments in the deepsea before the PETM could be due to non-preservation rather than nondeposition. For a discussion on the spatial and temporal trends in the distribution of the biosiliceous component in early Paleogene sediments of the Atlantic Ocean, albeit based on a different classification scheme, see Witkowski et al., 2020a.  

4.2. Carbonate sediment distribution at the PETM  

Sediment distribution at the PETM is dominated by clays (Fig. 3), reflecting wide-scale carbonate dissolution and CCD shoaling associated with significant carbon input to the atmosphere and oceans (Zachos et al., 2005). Diffusion of atmospheric CO2 into the oceans resulted in lowered pH and a concomitant increase in the rate of CaCO3 dissolu­ tion, temporarily ceasing carbonate deposition at many sites (Penman et al., 2014). The predominance of clay at the PETM also reflects a degree of ‘carbonate burndown’ (Griffith et al., 2015), wherein CaCO3 in the upper \~10–40 cm of the pelagic sedimentary cover underwent dissolution as more acidic deep ocean water percolated down through seafloor sediments (Kelly et al., 2010). Though carbonate ooze persists at some sites at the PETM (Fig. 3), this is largely an artefact of the broad sediment classification used herein, which conceals partial dissolution observed at all PETM carbonate sites, such as Site 690 where wt% CaCO3 decreased from > 76 to \~61% (Bralower et al., 2014).  

Comparison of Pacific Ocean (Shatsky Rise) and Atlantic Ocean (Walvis Ridge) sediment changes across the PETM reveals significant divergence in the dissolution response of each basin to C input. In the western Pacific, Shatsky Rise sites with paleodepths between 2000 and 3000 m maintain nannofossil ooze (Table 2) with CaCO3 wt% of > 75% (Colosimo et al., 2006). Conversely, Walvis Ridge sites with paleo­ depths in the range of 1500–3600 m have clay as the major lithology (Table 2) and contained virtually no carbonate at the PETM (Zachos et al., 2005). The divergent dissolution responses of the two major ocean basins reflect more extensive CCD shoaling in the Atlantic, where the PETM CCD occupied depths of \~1500 m, compared to \~3000 m in the Pacific (Zeebe and Zachos, 2007; Panchuk et al., 2008). Indeed, Atlantic CCD shoaling was so extensive that it extended up onto the shelf (Self-Trail et al., 2017; Bralower et al., 2018). This suggests an Atlantic source of PETM carbon, either from North Atlantic Igneous Province volcanism (Saunders, 2016), extensive methane hydrate destabilization or a combination of both, though circulation change has also been proposed to explain the variance between the Atlantic and Pacific CCD shoal (Zeebe and Zachos, 2007; Zeebe et al., 2009).  


There is an indication of meridional gradients in carbonate chem­ istry in the southern Atlantic. Comparison of Site 1263 (Walvis Ridge; 2717 m water depth) and Site 690 (Weddell Sea; 2914 m water depth) record markedly different dissolution responses to the PETM, despite their comparable paleodepths. The CCD shoaled by around 2000 m at Site 1263, causing dissolution of CaCO3 and a resultant clay layer with CaCO3 wt% of < 1% (Zachos et al., 2005); whilst the CCD remained below 2000 m at Site 690 with nannofossil ooze recorded in both the late Paleocene and PETM (Figs. 2 and 3, Table 2). The suggested gra­ dient in carbonate saturation from south to north is also evident in the comparatively expanded PETM section at Site 690, indicative of higher sediment input in the form of carbonate material and therefore less extensive dissolution at the PETM (Kelly et al., 2010). The presence of a latitudinal carbonate saturation gradient undermines the assertion that Atlantic meridional overturning temporarily reversed at the PETM (e.g., Bice and Maroatzke, 2002; Nunes and Norris, 2006). Had deep water formation switched from the Southern Ocean to the North Atlantic, waters with higher dissolved CO2 (a result of longer exposure to re­ spiring biota) would have bathed Site 690, whilst less corrosive waters would be expected at the more northerly Walvis Ridge.  

# 4.3. Biosiliceous sediment distribution at the PETM  

Penman (2016) suggested that increased weathering across the PETM would lead to enhanced silica availability and that porcellanite and chert are exceptionally common in the Eocene Atlantic Ocean (Penman et al., 2019). However, this is not reflected in our data set (Figs. 2 and 3). Chert is present at sites 702 and 748, with porcellanite at Site 959. Sites with poor recovery (which could be due to chert, e.g., sites 698, U1407) have not been included in our compilation, which most likely results in an underestimation of chert occurrences. While the low biosiliceous deposition may, to some extent, be an artefact of the classification scheme, we do not find extensive siliceous sedi­ mentation as seen in the modern and late Eocene sediment maps (Figs. 1 and 4), and the major modifier is predominately carbonate or clay (Figs. 2 and 3). Our study does not prohibit the enhanced silica burial as suggested by Penman et al. (2019) but does show that even with enhanced weathering and increased Si input, siliceous ooze is not the major modifier in most PETM sites. Enhanced biosiliceous sedi­ mentation does appear to be linked to PETM at Eurasian Platform sites, where extensive deposition of diatomite and diatom clays took place through much of the early Paleogene (e.g., Oreshkina, 2012; Oreshkina and Radionova, 2014).  

Site 959, located in the eastern equatorial Atlantic records porcella­ nite in all four time slices from the late Paleocene (\~56.5 Ma) to early Oligocene (\~33.5 Ma) (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 6). Sediments are also elevated in total organic carbon (Shipboard Scientific Party, 1996). As porcellanite is derived from recrystallization of biosiliceous opal-A to opal-CT, it sug­ gests that there was a sustained source of nutrients to this region throughout the early Paleogene, leading to high biosiliceous deposition. Nutrients may have been derived from upwelling or terrestrial influx. Cramwinckel et al. (2018) proposed that upwelling occurred in the middle and late Eocene at Site 959, based on the presence of Proto­ peridiniaceae cysts. However, our work suggests that upwelling/nutrient influx started before the late Paleocene, and continued into the Oligocene. In the modern ocean, biosiliceous sediments are a major constituent with diatomaceous ooze surrounding much of Antarctica (Dutkiewicz  

et al., 2015). One of the major findings of this paper is the doc­ umentation of the global paucity of biosiliceous sediments as the major modifier, compared to the modern ocean, despite both diatoms and radiolarians being ubiquitous in the early Paleogene (Witkowski et al., 2020a). There is no major turnover in radiolarians at the PETM, though Sanfilippo and Blome (2001) record a decrease in preservation and abundance. Does the absence of biosiliceous sedimentation as the major modifier, reflect a deficiency in biogenic silica production (e.g., a more diffuse nutrient regime), warm deep waters impacting biogenic silica preservation and burial, or a mixture of other mechanisms?  

The availability of macronutrients (nitrate and phosphate) and mi­ cronutrients (iron) is a major control on the distribution and abundance of diatoms. The low meridonial gradient, and lack of vertical mixing would have left limiting nutrients locked in the subsurface (Moore et al., 2008). Productivity proxies for the late Paleocene are rare and controversial, with most studies concentrating on the potential role of export productivity in the early Eocene as a mechanism of CO2 draw­ down (e.g., Paytan et al., 2007). Fontorbe et al. (2016, 2020) showed that oceanic dissolved silica is depleted throughout the water column in the North Atlantic during the PETM (see also Witkowski et al., 2020b for revised age model). Moore et al. (2008) also documented that si­ liceous deposits of the Eocene are less common than modern, sug­ gesting that silica input in the Eocene was lower. The deficiency of biogenic silica deposition around Antarctica at the PETM could, in part, be attributable to the sluggish circulation and inhibition of nutrient-rich upwelling across this interval (Heinze and Ilyina, 2015), and, on a more regional level, by the absence of a strong circumpolar current (Hill et al., 2013; Katz et al., 2011). Changes occurred soon after the PETM with cherts and siliceous microfossils preserved in the South Atlantic in the Eocene (Witkowski et al., 2020a).  

Equatorial divergence brings nutrient-rich waters to the surface where they are readily utilised by plankton. At the equator, the ap­ parent absence of biosiliceous sediments can also be linked to reduced upwelling and lack of nutrient supply associated with comparatively weaker trade winds resulting from diminished latitudinal temperature gradients (Moore et al., 2002, 2004; Lyle et al., 2008; Heinze and Ilyina, 2015). This effect would have been further compounded by warming across the PETM (Winguth et al., 2012).  

Diatom abundance and diversification through the Cenozoic can also explain the absence of biogenic silica-dominated sites across the PETM. It has been shown that, over geological timescales, diatom species diversity correlates with net biosiliceous sediment export pro­ ductivity (Lazarus, 2011). Though species turnover increased in re­ sponse to the PETM (Oreshkina, 2012), enhanced diversity at this time was largely the result of greater endemism, particularly at high lati­ tudes and in epicontinental seas (Lazarus et al., 2014; Barron et al., 2015). In the open oceans, diminished latitudinal temperature gra­ dients would have suppressed oceanic heterogeneity and therefore diatom species proliferation, precluding large-scale biogenic silica de­ position (Lazarus et al., 2014). Sims et al. (2006) proposed that diatoms transferred to the open ocean from the shelf through the Cenozoic. This view reconciles the rich fossil record from early Paleogene onshore sites, especially on the Eurasian Platform (Barron et al., 2015), with the scarcity of early Cenozoic diatoms in the deep-sea (Lazarus et al., 2014; Renaudie, 2016).  

The paucity of biosiliceous sediments in the early Paleogene may be attributed to dissolution. In the modern ocean, diatomaceous sediment deposition correlates with areas of low SSTs (< 6 °C) (Dutkiewicz et al., 2015). The solubility of biogenic silica is positively correlated with ocean temperature (Kamatani, 1982), and thus would more rapidly dissolve in the warmer ocean waters of the early Paleogene. Moreover, optimal diatom growth occurs where SSTs are significantly lower than those inferred for any sites during the late Paleocene/early Eocene (Neori and Holm-Hansen, 1982; Hollis et al., 2019). Further to this, recent studies have shown that diatom silicification is inhibited with high CO2 and thus lower pH (Petrou et al., 2019). The environmental conditions of the early Paleogene would have therefore influenced the distribution and preservation of siliceous organisms in the deep-sea realm.  

Finally, during diagenesis, biosilica may be mobilized and re­ deposited as chert nodules and beds, transferring the siliceous sedi­ ments to different horizons. Witkowski et al., 2020a show that the geographic distribution of lower Paleocene through upper Eocene chert and porcellanite in the Atlantic Ocean closely matches that of the bio­ genic siliceous sediments, which suggests that the picture of biogenic silica deposition in the early Cenozoic oceans is strongly biased due to diagenetic alteration.  

# 4.4. Sedimentary changes across the Eocene-Oligocene transition  

Global climate change across the EOT affects the sediment deposi­ tion pattern and is mainly characterised by the shift in the CCD. Calcareous sediments occur in both late Eocene and early Oligocene, however, the distribution in the early Oligocene is more extensive due to the change from non‑carbonate to carbonate deposition. The late Eocene CCD was relatively shallow at approximately 3300 m and varies between ocean basins (van Andel, 1975; Rea and Lyle, 2005; Pälike et al., 2012). The Pacific Ocean experienced the most dramatic CCD deepening (> 1000 m) (van Andel, 1975; Heath et al., 1977; Lyle et al., 2002; Rea and Lyle, 2005; Pälike et al., 2012) across the EOT.  

# 4.4.1. Carbonate compensation depth  

A prominent feature of the upper Eocene sediment map is the oc­ currence of radiolarian oozes in the eastern equatorial Pacific, which indicate the upwelling and advection of silicic acid and nutrients to surface waters in equatorial regions (Figs. 4 and 7b). This feature is referred to as the ‘equatorial tongue of high productivity’ (Moore et al., 2004). The distribution of siliceous sedimentation in the equatorial Pacific Ocean suggests that upwelling was initiated in the Eocene as latitudinal gradients increased. Radiolarian accumulation rates in the eastern equatorial Pacific decreased at the EOT (Moore et al., 2014), related to changes in ocean circulation (e.g., the source of the upwelled water at the equatorial divergence) impacting the nutrient concentra­ tion of advected subsurface waters (Funakawa et al., 2006; Moore et al., 2014).  

The signal of deepening of the CCD across the EOT is shown in the sediment distribution changes in most sites in the equatorial Pacific (ODP Leg 198, 199, IODP Leg 320) with the disappearance of siliceous sediments (Fig. 7). Radiolarian ooze in the eastern equatorial Pacific is surrounded by isolated areas of carbonate ooze, on the topographic highs, above the CCD. For example, Site 167 was drilled on the Ma­ gellan Rise, and Site 317 is located on the Manihiki Plateau, at water depths of 3200 m and 2600 m, respectively. The deepening of the CCD at the EOT allowed carbonate to be deposited at greater depth and caused a major composition change from radiolarian-dominated to calcareous-dominated sediments in the early Oligocene. Consequently, the distribution of siliceous sediments decreased. The early Oligocene equatorial Pacific sediments still contain a high abundance of siliceous organisms, however, silica is no longer the major modifier. Indeed, diatom deposition in the equatorial Pacific actually increases across the EOT (Moore et al., 2014), but the signal is masked by enhanced car­ bonate deposition.  

Apart from the CCD drop at the EOT, which changed the major modifier from radiolarian to carbonate ooze in the equatorial Pacific (Fig. 7), the disappearance of radiolarian oozes and decrease in radi­ olarian accumulation rates could be in part due to radiolarian faunal turnover related to the ocean cooling (Funakawa et al., 2006). Many species of radiolarians became extinct in a series of events during the middle and late Eocene and the EOT (Kamikuri and Wade, 2012; Moore and Kamikuri, 2012).  

Evidence of CCD deepening in not restricted to the equatorial Pacific Ocean. In the Atlantic and Indian oceans, the CCD also shows evidence of decline, but less severe than the Pacific (van Andel, 1975; Rea and Lyle, 2005 and references therein). The sedimentary change across the EOT in the Atlantic and Indian Ocean is relatively minor, as most sites are persistently located above the CCD through the transition. In the Atlantic (e.g., sites 1262 and 1267) and Indian oceans (sites 224 and 242) there is a change in sedimentation from non‑carbonate to carbo­ nate-dominated across the EOT. Changes from non‑carbonate to car­ bonate dominated deposition is recorded at sites 282, 1170 and 1171 in the Tasman Sea. Our depositional maps confirm that the CCD dee­ pening occurred on a global scale (van Andel, 1975; Peterson et al., 1992; Rea and Lyle, 2005). Some adjacent sites indicate no change owing to a greater paleodepth which was deeper than the CCD.  

The distribution of clay-dominated sediments during the EOT (as today) is generally dependent on ocean conditions that do not favour other types of sediment deposition, i.e., areas which are below the CCD and low productivity. Clay distribution at the EOT and in the modern ocean show a comparable distribution, though it is larger in the upper Eocene sediment map due to the shallower CCD, especially in the Atlantic Ocean.  

# 4.4.2. South Atlantic diatom ooze  

In the late Eocene and early Oligocene, the South Atlantic becomes an area for biogenic silica burial (Renaudie, 2016), with diatomdominated sediments found at sites 1090 and 511 (Figs. 4 and 6). Our sedimentary maps indicate that the conditions changed from the early Paleogene (Figs. 2 and 3) to the EOT (Figs. 4 and 6) to be more fa­ vourable to enhanced biosiliceous deposition in the South Atlantic (Figs. 3 and 4).  

Diatom deposition is generally considered to be an indicator of high surface water productivity (phosphorous, nitrogen, silica) (Barron and Baldauf, 1989), though Dutkiewicz et al. (2015) have recently ques­ tioned this. The presence of diatom oozes does not solely reflect diatom productivity as measured by opal accumulation rates, and sedimenta­ tion rates have a significant effect on diatom preservation. It has pre­ viously been suggested (e.g., Renaudie, 2016), that enhanced weath­ ering would have increased diatom deposition, due to higher silica availability. However, changes in ocean circulation likely drove diver­ gence and wind driven upwelling in the South Atlantic, redistributing nutrient supplies from the subsurface to the surface ocean, particularly silica, nitrate and phosphate and allowing the development of siliceous oozes. The diatomaceous sediments potentially represent greater ver­ tical mixing and intensified oceanic circulation (Miller et al., 2009; Houben et al., 2019b; Witkowski et al., 2020a), and the onset of an upwelling front (Plancq et al., 2014). A study from Site 1090 shows the occurrence of ‘opal pulse’ between late Eocene and earliest Oligocene which records the development of upwelling cells along topographic highs (Diekmann et al., 2004).  

Stickley et al. (2004) suggested that biogenic silica deposition began around 45 Ma along the Antarctic margin, and Egan et al. (2013) in­ dicated through silicon isotope (δ30Si) measurements, increased utili­ zation of silicic acid occurring from the late Eocene. Antarctic weath­ ering would have enhanced terrestrial-sourced micronutrients, i.e. iron, and relaxed iron limitation, allowing diatoms to thrive. Enhanced diatom abundance in the South Atlantic may be a potential mechanism for atmospheric CO2 drawdown in the middle and late Eocene through an increased biological pump (Rabosky and Sorhannus, 2009; Salamy and Zachos, 1999; Scher and Martin, 2006; Egan et al., 2013). New views on the relationship between climate and terrestrial silicate weathering are emerging (see Caves et al., 2016), and it remains to be verified how siliceous plankton production may have responded to these new scenarios.  

The distribution of diatom ooze does not significantly change across the EOT (Figs. 4 and 6), despite several studies indicating increased productivity in high southern latitudes (e.g. Site 744 and Site 689) (Diester-Haass, 1995; Diester-Haass et al., 1996; Salamy and Zachos, 1999; Latimer and Filippelli, 2002; Diester-Haass and Zahn, 2001;  

Diekmann et al., 2004; Anderson and Delaney, 2005; Plancq et al., 2014). There is evidence of increased abundance of diatoms (e.g., Site 744, Salamy and Zachos, 1999), however, carbonate remains the major modifier. We do not see the enhancement in productivity reflected in biosiliceous sediment deposition. Sediments close to Antarctica in the early Oligocene (e.g., Kerguelen Plateau and Tasman Sea) are carbo­ nate, not siliceous oozes.  

Surface water circulation patterns were altered by tectonic evolu­ tion and the opening and closing of gateways. The opening of the Drake Passage and the initiation of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) is controversial, with estimates ranging from the middle Eocene to late Oligocene (Scher and Martin, 2006; Pfuhl and McCave, 2005; Katz et al., 2011; Scher et al., 2015). Recent modelling studies have sug­ gested that productivity changes in response to the Drake Passage opening would have varied spatially (Ladant et al., 2018), with diatoms predominating at high latitudes. The sediment distribution in the late Eocene and early Oligocene does not change and thus does not support that the Drake Passage opened, or that the proto-ACC was initiated across the Eocene/Oligocene boundary.  

We do not see any change or expansion biosiliceous depositional patterns across the EOT, indicating that the mechanisms to allow en­ hanced diatom deposition in the South Atlantic were in place before the end of the Eocene. The small number of early Oligocene diatomaceous dominated sites may also be an artefact of the distribution of ocean drilling sites. Of course, carbon export can increase without a change in biosiliceous sediment distribution, and recent modelling investigations by Ladant et al. (2018) indicate an increase in export production in the South Atlantic whilst diatom primary production decreases. The re­ mineralization of organic matter through the water column is tem­ perature dependent (John et al., 2013), thus providing a mechanism to resolve the conflict here between sediment and productivity records.  

The sediment distribution pattern of diatom-dominated sediments during the EOT is markedly different from modern oceans (Figs. 1, 4 and 6). Diatom oozes are not abundant at the EOT, and are only found in the South Atlantic (Figs. 4 and 6). Sites 1090 and 511 were located 47oS and 54oS, respectively, at the EOT (Table 3), very different to the current area of diatomaceous sediment accumulation in the modern ocean, where diatom-dominated sediments are highly concentrated in the circum-Antarctic belt (Dutkiewicz et al., 2015). At higher latitudes (> 55S), deposition at the EOT is predominately carbonate e.g., Site 277, 738, 744, 748, 749, 1137, 1170, 1171 with a few clay dominated sites. We do not see find the extensive diatom oozes that are seen in the modern ocean (Fig. 1). Increased global cooling during the Eocene developed the strong differentiation between the high and low latitudes which invigorated oceanic circulation and enhanced biosiliceous sedi­ mentation in the South Atlantic (Baldauf and Barron, 1990; Miller et al., 2009). Progressive climatic deterioration in the Neogene increased the thermal gradient, resulting in the geographically restricted upwelling regions observed today and development of the opal belt around Ant­ arctica (Baldauf and Barron, 1990; Cortese et al., 2004).  

# 4.5. Glaciomarine deposition at the EOT  

The most significant event of the EOT is the appearance of a con­ tinental-scale ice sheet on Antarctica and glaciomarine sediments are the most direct evidence. Some cores indicate the onset of glaciation on Antarctica during the EOT, through the presence of ice-rafted debris (IRD) and glacial diamictites (Wise et al., 1991; Wise et al., 1992; Breza and Wise, 1992; Zachos et al., 1992; Ehrmann and Mackensen, 1992; Ivany et al., 2006; Scher et al., 2014). Sites 696, 738, 742, 744 and 748 contain IRD but as a minor component (Carter et al., 2017; Barron et al., 1991); Site 742 is not complete and thus has been excluded from our compilation. In the ocean sediment distribution maps of the late Eocene and early Oligocene (Figs. 4 and 6), diamictite occurs near the Antarctic continent at Site 739, located in Prydz Bay, East Antarctica, implying that glaciers in this area reached sea level during late Eocene.  

In the earliest Oligocene, despite widespread evidence of an Antarctic ice sheet, we do not find glaciomarine sediments as the major modifier at any other DSDP, ODP or IODP site.  

# 5. Conclusions­­­­­­­  

We compiled ocean sedimentary maps of the late Paleocene, PETM, late Eocene and early Oligocene, based on sediment lithology records from DSDP, ODP and IODP, to document the sedimentary changes across two of the most significant climate events in the Cenozoic. Our tables provide a catalogue of all existing ocean drilling sites covering these intervals drilled to date. In mapping sediment distribution and composition, we provide a graphic synthesis of the extent of climatic change as recorded in the ocean sediment cores. Sedimentary changes across the PETM, chiefly the emergence of clays as the major sediment type and the dissolution of carbonates are evidence of widespread shoaling of the CCD. Our study also illustrates how the deposition of siliceous sediments altered through the early Paleogene. During the latest Paleocene and PETM, biogenic silica deposition was low in comparison to the modern. Diminished biosiliceous deposition is explained in the context of a more diffuse upwelling regime which persisted across the interval of study, coupled with reduced diatom abundance and dissolution of silica in the warm early Paleogene oceans. For the late Paleocene, carbonate sediments are shown to have been pervasive, extending to far southerly latitudes and this is commensurate with the warm, oligotrophic ocean state. Across the EOT, the CCD dramatically deepened, manifesting in a shift at many locations, from non-calcareous to calcareous dominated sediments. The prominent feature of the late Eocene map is the tongue of radiolarian oozes in the eastern equatorial Pacific which relate to shallower CCD and equatorial divergence. Our depositional maps confirm the global scale deepening of the CCD across the EOT. In the Eocene South Atlantic, diatom dominated sediments develop but the pattern differs from the modern  distribution,  with  carbonate  rich  sediments  bordering Antarctica, unlike the glacial and siliceous rich sediments seen today.  

The maps of sediment data help to indicate and visualise past ocean conditions and contribute to the understanding of biogeochemical cycles in the ocean in response to the climate change. Undoubtedly, as more ocean drilling data becomes available, more accurate mapping of the seafloor sediment can be achieved with improved resolution and coverage.
