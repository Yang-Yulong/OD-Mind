# a b s t r a c t  

Integrated Ocean Drilling Program (IODP) Site U1308 (49 530N, 24 140W; water depth 3871 m) provides a record of relative paleointensity (RPI) and benthic stable isotope stratigraphy back to 3.2 Ma. The record since 1.5 Ma was published previously, and here we present the interval from 1.5 Ma to 3.2 Ma (Early Pleistocene-Late Pliocene). The benthic oxygen isotope record in this interval is correlated to Marine Isotope Stage (MIS) 51 to KM2, with an apparent hiatus that removed part of the interval spanning MIS 104-G2 (2.6e2.65 Ma), at the Gauss-Matuyama boundary. The mean sedimentation rate for the 1.5e3.2 Ma period is 8.5 cm/kyr. The age model was built by correlation of the benthic oxygen isotope record to a global stack (LR04). Apart from the expected polarity reversals, three magnetic excursions are recorded: Punaruu in MIS 31/32 at 1092 ka, Gilsa in MIS 54/55 at 1584 ka, and a newly recognized excursion labeled Porcupine (after the nearby Porcupine Abyssal Plain) in MIS G6/G7 at 2737 ka. The ages of polarity reversals at Site U1308, on the LR04 time scale, are consistent with the current geomagnetic polarity timescale (GPTS) with the exception of the base of the Olduvai Subchron that occurred in MIS 73, corresponding to 1925 ka on the LR04 time scale, 25 kyr younger than in the current GPTS. The RPI record at Site U1308 is calibrated using the oxygen isotope age model, and combined with four other North Atlantic records to obtain a North Atlantic RPI stack for 1.2e2.2 Ma (NARPI-2200) that is compared with published RPI stacks: Epapis, Sint-2000 and PADM2M. For 2.2e3.2 Ma, the Site U1308 RPI record is compared with a RPI record from North Atlantic IODP Site U1314, and with the Pacific Epapis stack. The mean sedimentation rates of the North Atlantic sites in NARPI-2200 are greater (by about an order of magnitude) than most of the records incorporated in other stacks. The comparison of Epapis and NARPI2200 yields an apparent lag for NARPI-2200 relative to Epapis, attributed the Epapis age model constructed by correlation of magnetic concentration parameters (a proxy for carbonate percentage) to a calibrated oxygen isotope record. The long RPI record from Site U1308 yields a very similar mean value for the Brunhes and Matuyama virtual axial dipole moments (7.05  1022 Am2), implying no polarity bias in the strength of the main geomagnetic dipole, in contrast to interpretations from Sint-2000 and PADM2M. The results strengthen the case that RPI can be used to improve global stratigraphic correlation for sites with mean sedimentation rates up to several decimeters/kyr.  

# 1. Introduction  

Integrated Ocean Drilling Program (IODP) Site U1308 (49 530 N, 24 140W) was drilled in 2004 in a water depth of 3871 m (Fig. 1). It is a reoccupation of Deep Sea Drilling Project (DSDP) Site 609, drilled in 1983. Construction of a complete composite section at DSDP Site 609 was compromised by intervals of poor recovery (Ruddiman et al., 1987). Six holes were drilled at Site U1308 and a composite splice was constructed shipboard to 248 m composite depth (mcd) (Expedition 303 Scientists, 2006). The composite splice was then modified from its shipboard version in the upper 103 mcd (Hodell et al., 2008) and, for this paper, we use a mcd-scale amended to follow the modified version. Shipboard paleomagnetic measurements (Expedition 303 Scientists, 2006) demonstrated that the recovered section at Site U1308 reaches to just below the base of the Mammoth Subchronozone at \~264 m composite depth (mcd), although the composite splice ends at 248 mcd, just below the base of the Kaena Subchronozone. Post-cruise magnetic measurements using u-channel samples, continuous 2  2  150 cm3 samples encased in plastic, collected from each core section within the composite splice, extend to just below the Kaena Subchronozone to 248 mcd, equivalent to 3.15 Ma. Below this depth, the sediments were too indurated for efficient u-channel sampling.  

Benthic stable isotope data, and relative paleointensity (RPI) data, from the upper 109 mcd (last 1.5 Ma) at Site U1308 have been published (Hodell et al., 2008; Channell et al., 2008). The Site U1308 RPI/isotope records were used as the target for the generation of the PISO-1500 paleointensity stack for the last 1500 kyrs that utilized 13 records, mainly but not entirely from the North Atlantic, which have both oxygen isotope data and RPI associated with them (Channell et al., 2009). For PISO-1500, the RPI and oxygen isotope records from the 12 sites were optimally correlated in tandem to the RPI and oxygen isotope records of Site U1308, using the Match protocol (Lisiecki and Lisiecki, 2002). The PISO stack therefore utilizes the Site U1308 age model. Here, we present Site U1308 RPI/ d18O records for 1.5e3.2 Ma and generate a RPI stack for 1.2e2.2 Ma (NARPI-2200) from five available North Atlantic records, which is then compared with existing RPI stacks.  

DSDP Site 609 has been important for studies of benthic d18O, d13C and %CaCO3 for the Pleistocene and late Pliocene and the interpretation of these records in terms of ice sheet variability and oceanic circulation, and for producing orbitally-tuned timescales integrated with magnetic polarity stratigraphy (Ruddiman et al., 1986, 1989; Raymo et al., 1989). Also at this site, early recognition of detrital (Heinrich-type) layers led to the correlation of sea surface temperature minima to cold stadials in the d18O from the  

Summit (Greenland) ice core record and to the grouping of DansgaardeOeschger (ice core) cycles into Bond cycles where each cycle is terminated by the massive iceberg discharges from Hudson Strait that constitute Heinrich events (Bond et al., 1993, 1999). The purpose of this paper is to integrate the magnetic stratigraphy and oxygen isotope stratigraphy at Site U1308 prior to 1.5 Ma, and produce a high-resolution temporally calibrated RPI record for 1.5e3.2 Ma.  

# 2. Methods  

# 2.1. Magnetic methods  

Core sections within the composite splice at Site U1308 were sampled using u-channels to the base of the composite section at 248 mcd. For this paper, the natural remanent magnetization (NRM), of u-channel samples collected from the 110e248 mcd interval, was measured at 1-cm intervals on a u-channel cryogenic magnetometer at the University of Florida, with a 10-cm leader/ trailer, as the sample was tracked through the measurement space. Each 1-cm measurement is not independent of adjacent measurements due to the \~4.5-cm width at half-height of the magnetometer response function (see Weeks et al., 1993; Guyodo et al., 2002). After initial NRM measurement, stepwise AF demagnetization was carried out at 5 mT intervals from 10 to 80 mT, and then 10 mT steps to 100 mT. Component magnetizations were determined for a uniform 20e80 mT demagnetization interval, at each 1-cm measurement step, using the standard procedure (Kirschvink, 1980) and the software of Xuan and Channell (2009). The maximum angular deviation (MAD) values provide a measure of the definition of each determination of the component magnetization direction, with values <5  indicating high quality data. After NRM measurements, the volume magnetic susceptibility (k) of u-channel samples was measured at 1-cm spacing using a susceptibility track designed for u-channels (Thomas et al., 2003).  


The principal behind relative paleointensity (RPI) proxies is that variations in magnetizing field strength at the time of NRM acquisition, can be determined by normalizing the NRM intensity by the intensity of a laboratory-induced magnetization that activates the same population of grains that carry NRM, thereby compensating for changes in concentration of NRM-carrying grains down-core (see Banerjee and Mellema, 1974; Levi and Banerjee, 1976; Tauxe, 1993). The normalizer may be anhysteretic remanent magnetization (ARM) or isothermal remanent magnetization (IRM), and the method is only applicable if the magnetization is carried by fine-grained (pseudo-single domain or single domain) magnetite or titanomagnetite, with grain sizes <\~10e20 mm.  


For this study, ARM was acquired using a 50 mT DC bias field, and IRM was acquired in a 0.3 T and then a 1 T field. The ARM normalizer activates a finer population of magnetite grains than IRM, so the choice of effective normalizer depends on the grain-size distribution of the magnetite. In practice, we use both normalizers (ARM and IRM) and carry out both ARM acquisition (ARMAQ) and ARM/IRM demagnetization (e.g. Channell et al., 2014). For three methods of normalization (NRM/ARM, NRM/ARMAQ and NRM/ IRM), we determine slopes in a chosen demagnetization/acquisition peak AF field interval (in this case 20e60 mT), and determine the linear correlation coefficient (r) at 1-cm intervals associated with each of three slopes (see Channell et al., 2002, 2014). Agreement amongst the three normalized remanence ratios may improve confidence in the RPI proxies. Note that the RPI proxies yield relative paleointensity estimates, and must be calibrated using absolute paleointensity determinations, to yield virtual axial dipole moments (VADM).  

The anhysteretic susceptibility (kARM) is the ARM intensity divided by the DC bias field used to generate it (in our case: 50 mT). The ratio kARM/k can be used as a proxy for magnetite grain size (King et al., 1983), and uniformity in the ratio is an indicator of uniformity in magnetite grain size that is an important criterion for generating useful RPI data. Using a Princeton Measurements Corp. vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM), we measured small (toothpick) samples in #4 gel-caps (N  71), from each u-channel sample, to determine the hysteresis parameters. These measurements yield a Day et al. (1977) diagram of hysteresis ratios, also indicative of magnetite grain size.  

# 2.2. Stable isotope methods  

After completion of magnetic measurements, the u-channel samples were sub-sampled for stable isotope analyses, with authorization from IODP. Sediment was bagged in 5-cm aliquots from the square 2  2 cm cross-section of the u-channels, resulting in 30 samples from each 150-cm u-channel. These 20 cm samples, from each 5-cm stratigraphic interval of the u-channels, were washed and sieved at 63 mm. Foraminifer specimens were picked from the >212-mm size fraction, and one to five individuals were used for analysis. Stable isotopes were measured on the benthic foraminifer Cibicidoides wuellerstorfi and/or Cibicidoides kullenbergi at a sample spacing of 5e10 cm for the 100e248 mcd interval of Site U1308. Foraminifer tests were soaked in \~15% H2O2 for 30 min to remove organic matter. The tests were then rinsed with methanol and sonically cleaned to remove fine-grained particles. The methanol was removed with a syringe, and samples were dried in an oven at 50  C for 24 h. The foraminifer calcite was loaded into individual reaction vessels, and each sample was reacted with three drops of H3PO4 (specific gravity ¼ 1.92) using a Finnigan MAT Kiel III carbonate preparation device. Isotope ratios were measured online by a Finnigan MAT 252 mass spectrometer at the University of Florida. Analytical precision was estimated to be ± 0.08‰ for d18O by measuring eight standards (NBS-19) with each set of 38 samples. A total of 1708 samples of Cibicidoides wuellerstorfi and/or Cibicidoides kullenbergi were measured for this study.  

# 3. Results  

# 3.1. Stable isotopes  

The oxygen isotope data for Site U1308 in the 100e250 mcd interval (Fig. 2) display glacialeinterglacial cycles that are interpreted to represent MIS 44 to KM2. The Site U1308 d18O data from MIS 50 to recent have been published previously (Hodell et al., 2008). Bailey et al. (2010) measured d18O for Site U1308 samples in the vicinity of the Gauss-Matuyama (G/M) boundary (MIS 100-G8, red symbols in the d18O record of Fig. 2), and their identification of isotopic stages is entirely consistent with the interpretation given here. Compared to our d18O values, the higher values of d18O from Bailey et al. (2010) are due to the addition of 0.64‰ that was applied to adjust their data to equilibrium calcite to compensate for the species used (Cibicidoides wuellerstorfi) that secretes calcite out of equilibrium with seawater (Shackleton and Hall, 1984). In Bailey et al. (2010), the G/M boundary was placed in a 4 m interval at 195.5e199.5 mcd (adjusted to mcd-scale of Hodell et al., 2008) based on shipboard magnetic data (Expedition 303 Scientists, 2006), and MIS 100e102 and MIS G4-G7 are recorded (see Fig. 4 of Bailey et al., 2010). We can now revise the placement of the G/ M boundary to \~196e197 mcd (Table 2); however, the G/M boundary interval is complicated by low sedimentation rates and syn-sedimentary deformation. It appears, from our data, that part of the MIS 104-G2 interval was removed resulting in a hiatus between \~2.6 and 2.65 Ma (Fig. 2). Support for a hiatus in this interval comes from digital image scans of core sections from 195 to 198 mcd of the composite splice (sections U1308F-19H-3 to 5) that provide evidence of slumping in the vicinity of 197 mcd (Fig. 3).  



U-channel samples collected from the composite splice provide overlap at ties between holes within the composite splice, because, if part of the core section was within the composite splice, we were permitted to sample the entire core section. This allowed us to test three ties within the composite splice that were deemed less secure during shipboard composite section construction (Expedition 303 Scientists, 2006). The ties at 193.65 mcd (adjusted mcd to Hodell et al., 2008) between Holes U1308A and U1308F, at 210.61 mcd between Holes U1308F and U1308C, and at 229.63 mcd between Holes U1308F and U1308C, were tested, and the overlapping isotope data support these hole-to-hole correlations.  

# 3.2. Rock magnetism  

The rock magnetism of the upper part of the section at Site U1308 (<1.5 Ma) indicates that magnetite is the dominant magnetic mineral (Channell et al., 2008). In Fig. 2, we also plot three magnetic concentration parameters, intensity of ARM and IRM (after demagnetization at peak fields of 30 mT) and volume susceptibility, with the ratio of anhysteretic susceptibility to susceptibility (kARM/ k), a grain size proxy for magnetite. In general, glacial isotopic stages are associated with higher magnetic concentration (high values of ARM, IRM and susceptibility) and a tendency for coarser grain sizes (lower values of kARM/k) (Fig. 2). A long-term decrease in kARM/k from 250 mcd to \~190 mcd can be associated with increased detrital (magnetite) grain size during the intensification of Northern Hemisphere Glaciation (NHG), although the overall range of the kARM/k ratio indicates a restricted range of magnetite grain size. The distribution of points on a kARM versus k plot indicates highly uniform magnetite grain size (Fig. 4a), and can be associated with a particular magnetite grain size (King et al., 1983). The grain size estimates are unrealistically low (<0.1 mm) unless the ARM intensity after AF demagnetization is plotted (Fig. 4a). A peak demagnetization field of 30 mT for ARM yields an estimate of \~1 mm for the mean magnetite grain size, consistent with the estimate from the Day et al. (1977) hysteresis ratio plot (Fig. 4b) in which samples lie in the pseudo-single domain (PSD) field in a region associated with mean magnetite grain sizes of \~1 mm. The restricted scatter of hysteresis ratios on the magnetite grain-size mixing line in Fig. 4b (mixing line adopted from Dunlop and Carter-Stiglitz, 2006), and the calibration of this mixing line using sized unannealed magnetite grains (Dunlop, 2002), provide estimates of “mean” magnetite grain size.  

# 3.3. Natural remanent magnetization (NRM)  

NRM magnetization components were determined for a uniform (20e80 mT) demagnetization range, and anchoring at the origin of orthogonal projections is unnecessary due to the progression of data points to the origin of the projection. The use of a uniform demagnetization interval for determination of component magnetization directions allows a straight-forward assessment of data quality through maximum angular deviation (MAD) values that are largely below 5 (Fig. 5), indicating well defined magnetization components. Declinations were corrected by uniform rotation of individual cores so that mean declinations of cores point North or South for positive and negative inclination intervals, respectively. Although the “tensor” core orientation tool was used for many individual cores, it was found to be sporadically unreliable and therefore was not employed here. The polarity pattern (Fig. 5) is compatible with the interpretation of the younger part of the Site U1308 record (<1.5 Ma) that records the Matuyama/Brunhes (MB) boundary, the Jaramillo and Cobb Mt. subchrons, and the Punaruu excursion (Channell et al., 2008). In the 100e270 mcd interval, the Olduvai and Re union subchronozones, the Gauss/Matuyama (G/M) boundary and the Kaena Subchronozone are recognized. Shipboard paleomagnetic data in the 250e270 mcd interval (Fig. 5), after demagnetization at a single peak field of 10 mT, led to tentative identification of the Mammoth Subchronozone (Expedition 303 Scientists, 2006). Erratic component magnetization directions, particularly apparent in inclination values, at the G/M boundary are attributed to the interval of slumping (Fig. 3) that may also account of the missing isotopic stages in the MIS 104-G2 interval (Fig. 2). Two magnetic excursions are apparently recorded: the Gilsa Excursion at 117 mcd that has been recognized elsewhere (Channell et al., 2002), and a newly recognized excursion just below the G/M boundary at 204 mcd, named Porcupine excursion after the nearby Porcupine Abyssal Plain. The Porcupine excursion is recorded by uchannel samples from the archive halves of two core sections from different holes (U1308C-20H-5 and U1308F-20H-2), and by discrete samples from the working half of U1308F-20H-2 (Fig. 5).

# 3.4. Relative paleointensity (RPI)  

NRM/ARM, NRM/ARMAQ and NRM/IRM provide relative paleointensity (RPI) proxies determined as slopes calculated for a uniform 20e60 mT demagnetization/acquisition interval, at 1-cm intervals along the composite splice. Linear correlation coefficients (r) were calculated for each slope as a measure of their definition (Fig. 7). The linear correlation coefficients are lower (less than 1) for NRM/IRM slopes than for NRM/ARM or NRM/ARMAQ slopes, indicating less well defined NRM/IRM slopes, particularly in the 125e150 mcd interval. This is also the interval in which magnetic concentration parameters indicate enhanced magnetic concentration, from high values of ARM and IRM intensity and susceptibility, and coarser magnetic grain size from lower values of kARM/k (Fig. 2). In general, the three RPI proxies (NRM/ARM, NRM/ ARMAQ and NRM/IRM) are consistent (Fig. 7). Bivariate plots of the NRM/ARM slopes versus ARM intensity and kARM/k indicate no statistically significant relationship between the RPI proxy (NRM/ ARM) and the normalizer (ARM) and the magnetite grain size proxy (Fig. 8). The NRM/ARM slope is used as the RPI proxy for Site U1308, and incorporated in the North Atlantic RPI stack (NARPI-2200).  

# 4. Site U1308 age model  

The Site U1308 age model was constructed by correlation of the benthic oxygen isotope data to the LR04 benthic oxygen isotope stack (Lisiecki and Raymo, 2005) using 50 tie points (Table 1) with linear interpolation between adjacent tie points. The age model is consistent with the younger (<1.5 Ma) part of the Site U1308 record (Hodell et al., 2008), and uses an analogous procedure through correlation to LR04. For MIS 51-93 (1500e2400 ka), the age model indicates sedimentation rates in the 3e18 cm/kyr range with a minimum in glacial stage MIS 70 and a maximum in interglacial MIS 93 (Fig. 9). For the MIS 95-KM2 (2400e3150 ka) interval, very low sedimentation rates occur in MIS 96, MIS 100, the MIS 104-G2 interval and MIS G12; with a maximum sedimentation rate in MIS G13 (Fig. 9). Sedimentation rates prior to 3125 ka are derived from shipboard paleomagnetic measurements (Expedition 303 Scientists, 2006) that allow tentative identification of the Mammoth Subchronozone (Fig. 5). We observe a progressive increase in d18O from \~2.6‰ in MIS K1 to \~3.5‰ in MIS 99-100, and thereafter, as global ice volume progressively increased during the intensification of NHG. The step increase in benthic d18O at \~MIS 100, highlighted by Bailey et al. (2010) from LR04, is less apparent in our data. As mentioned above, the resulting age model includes a hiatus that appears to have removed part of the MIS 104-G2 interval (Fig. 9), at the G/M boundary, attributed to apparent slumping (Fig. 3). The ties from Site U1308 to LR04 (Table 1) were determined without reference to the polarity stratigraphy; therefore, the apparent age of polarity reversals (Table 2) provides a check on the age model.  

# 5. The geomagnetic polarity timescale (GPTS)  

Prior to 1990, the consensus GPTS for the last 2.5 Myrs was based on KeAr radiometric ages from lavas that recorded polarity reversals of the Brunhes and Matuyama chrons (e.g., Mankinen and Daltrymple, 1979). Shackleton et al. (1990) were the first to demonstrate that these ages were 5e7% too old, from astronomical tuning of the ODP Site 677 planktic and benthic d18O records. Polarity reversals were not recorded at ODP Site 677, and the ages of reversals in Shackleton et al. (1990) were determined by correlation of the d18O record of ODP Site 677 to DSDP Site 607, which possessed both an oxygen isotope and polarity reversal stratigraphy (Raymo et al.,1989; Clement and Robinson,1987). The ages of polarity reversals provided by Shackleton et al. (1990) have stood for 25 years, supported by corroborating ages determined from cyclostratigraphy of Mediterranean sapropels (Hilgen, 1991a), and are adopted with only minor modifications in current versions of the GPTS (Lourens et al., 2004; Ogg, 2012) (Table 2).

Lisiecki and Raymo (2005), referred to as LR04, estimated the age of reversals back to the G/M boundary by determining the mean ages (Table 2) at eight DSDP/ODP sites (607, 659, 677, 982, 983, 1090, 1146, 1148) incorporated in the LR04 isotope stack, with standard deviations of mean values in the (undifferentiated) 5e22 kyr range. Only three of these sites (Sites 607, 982, 983) carry adequate magnetic stratigraphies. Apart from Site 677, which has no polarity stratigraphy, other sites (Sites 659, 1090, 1146 and 1148) have poorly-resolved polarity stratigraphies mainly based on limited shipboard demagnetization treatment (Tauxe et al., 1989; Shipboard Scientific Party, 1999; Wang et al., 2000).  

Uncertainties in ages of reversals in LR04 are due to uncertainties in the position of the reversal relative to the marine isotope stratigraphy, rather than uncertainties in the ice-volume model (Imbrie and Imbrie, 1980) that provides the ages for LR04. Ice-volume model uncertainties are, however, important in assessing uncertainties in the Site U1308 age model that relies on LR04. Errors in ice-volume forcing in LR04, insolation at 65 N from Laskar et al. (1993), can be attributed to uncertainties in the tidal dissipation terms in the insolation calculation, and they are unlikely to be more than a few kyr at 3 Ma (see Lourens et al., 2004). There are uncertainties in the values for nonlinearity (b) and time constant (Tm) in the ice-volume calculation, which in the case of LR04 vary linearly from b ¼ 0.3 and Tm¼ 5 kyr at 3.0 Ma, to b ¼ 0.6 and Tm  15 kyr at 1.5 Ma, with constant values since that time. Likely ranges for b and Tm contribute to uncertainties in the ice-volume  

# model of a few kyr at the Matuyama-Brunhes boundary (see Channell et al., 2010).  

For the younger part (<1.5 Ma) of the Site U1308 record (Channell et al., 2008), ages of polarity reversals and their correlation to MIS are consistent with Shackleton et al. (1990) for the bounds of the Jaramillo and the Cobb Mountain subchrons (Fig. 10 and Table 2). Shackleton et al. (1990) provided a single age (1190 ka) for the Cobb Mt. Subchron, whereas a 21 kyr duration (1187e1208 ka) is resolved at Site U1308, and the age is \~10e15 kyr older than in the current GPTS (Table 2). A magnetic excursion interpreted to be the Punaruu excursion (Fig.10, Table 2), has an age of 1092 ka at the MIS 31/32 transition at Site U1308 that is \~23 kyr younger than the age for this excursion determined at ODP Site 983/984 (1115 ka, Channell et al., 2002), and younger than the age for this excursion determined from 40Ar/39Ar radiometric dating of lavas (1105 ka, Singer et al., 1999) that record the excursion (Chauvin et al., 1990).  

The Site U1308 age model yields ages for polarity reversals that are consistent with the current GPTS, for the end/top of the Olduvai Subchron but not for the onset/base of the Olduvai Subchron (Fig. 11, Table 2). For the age of the top of the Olduvai, Shackleton et al. (1990) correlated MIS 63 at DSDP Site 607, where the top Olduvai was recorded (in the boxed interval of Fig. 11c), to the ODP Site 677 astrochronology. At Site 607, the base of the Olduvai Subchronozone is at 81.68e82.46 mcd (within the boxed interval of Fig.11c), within MIS 73 according to Raymo et al. (1989). Shackleton et al. (1990) chose, however, to assign the base Olduvai to MIS 71 and 1950 ka, although MIS 71 would now have an age < 1900 ka according to LR04 (Lisiecki and Raymo, 2005). The Shackleton et al. (1990) MIS/age designation for the base of the Olduvai does not follow the Site 677/607 d18O correlation procedure used for determining the age of other Quaternary reversals, but was based on the association of the base Olduvai with the extinction of Discoaster brouweri, and the association of this extinction event with MIS 71 at DSDP Site 607 (Ruddiman et al., 1986) and at other locations (Backman and Shackleton, 1983). Lisiecki and Raymo (2005) placed the base Olduvai in MIS 74 at 1968 ka based on the averaging procedure mentioned above. At Site U1308, however, the base  


Olduvai clearly lies in MIS 73 at \~1925 ka on the LR04 time scale (Fig. 11, Table 2), which is 26e44 kyr younger than the ages predicted by Shackleton et al. (1990), Hilgen (1991a), Lisiecki and Raymo (2005), and those incorporated in current versions of the GPTS (Lourens et al., 2004; Ogg, 2012).  

Although the base of the Olduvai was placed in MIS 74 at 1968 ka in LR04 (Lisiecki and Raymo, 2005), there are numerous examples of the base Olduvai being placed in MIS 71 and/or at \~1950 ka, although, as mentioned above, MIS 71 no longer corresponds to 1950 ka and has an age < 1900 ka according to LR04. The onset of the Olduvai Subchron was correlated to MIS 71 in Core MD972143 (Horng et al., 2002), ODP Sites 981 and 984 (Channell et al., 2003, 2002), although in all three cases, MIS 71 appears to have been misidentified, perhaps due to the “reinforcement syndrome” provided by the designation of Shackleton et al. (1990). At ODP Site 659, where the d18O record is well defined (Fig. 11d, Tiedemann et al., 1994), the base of the Olduvai was again placed in MIS 71 although the subchron boundary is poorly defined at this site (Tauxe et al., 1989). In Italian land sections, the base of the Olduvai was placed at 1950 ka (Hilgen, 1991a) within MIS 71 (Lourens et al., 1992, 1996) although the polarity subchron boundary is poorly defined in the relevant land sections (Monte Singa and Vrica) in southern Italy (Zijderveld et al., 1991), due to complex remagnetizations attributed to authigenic iron sulfides (Roberts et al., 2010). At North Pacific ODP Site 882, the age of the base Olduvai was also placed at 1.95 Ma, based on astrochronology utilizing orbital power in the GRAPE density data (Tiedemann and Haug, 1995).  



A single Re union Subchron is recognized at Site U1308 in the MIS 79-81 interval (Fig. 11, Table 2). The identification of a single normal polarity R eunion Subchron, rather than a doubled normal subchron (e.g., Mankinen and Daltrymple, 1979) is consistent with the result from ODP Site 982 (Channell and Guyodo, 2004) and ODP Site 981 (Channell et al., 2003). The duration of the R eunion Subchron at Site U1308 (21 kyr) is, however, about half that estimated at ODP Site 981 (38 kyr). The Gilsa excursion at Site U1308 occurs in MIS 54/55 at \~1584 ka (Fig. 11, Table 2), consistent with its age at ODP Sites 983 and 984 (Channell et al., 2002).  


Although the G/M boundary at Site U1308 (Fig. 5) is affected by slumping (Fig. 3), the estimated age for the G/M boundary at Site U1308 is consistent with the age given by Shackleton et al. (1990) and Hilgen (1991a) and incorporated in the current GPTS (Table 2). The age and MIS designation for the Kaena Subchron at Site U1308 (Fig. 12, Table 2) differs from the assignment given by Lisiecki and Raymo (2005) and is more consistent with Hilgen (1991a,b). The apparent excursion at 2737 ka within MIS G6/G7 (Fig. 12, Table 2), labeled Porcupine Excursion, lies well below the interval affected by slumping (Fig. 3).  

# 6. Atlantic RPI stack  

At Site U1308, the three relative paleointensity (RPI) proxies, slopes of NRM/ARM, NRM/ARMAQ and NRM/IRM are often consistent (Fig. 7), but the categorical test of the Site U1308 RPI record must come from comparison with RPI records from other sites. For the 1.5e3.2 Ma interval, there are few RPI records for comparison. Because the non-axial dipole (NAD) components of the geomagnetic field have centennial-scale repeat times, based on analysis of the recent field (Lhuillier et al., 2011; Valet et al., 2008), RPI data recovered from sediments with mean sedimentation rates < \~50 cm/kyr are generally thought to represent the main axial dipole and therefore be globally correlative.

# 7. Discussion  

The GPTS for the last 3.2 Myr has remained essentially unchanged since the temporal calibration of polarity reversals provided 25 years ago by Shackleton et al. (1990) and Hilgen (1991a,b). As the d18O record at Site U1308 has been interpreted irrespective of the magnetic stratigraphy, the age model provides a check on the GPTS for the last 3.2 Myrs. The ages and MIS assignments of polarity reversals are largely confirmed by this work (Table 2, Figs. 10e12), however, the onset of the Olduvai is at 1925 ka, within MIS 73 (Fig. 11), rather than the traditional assignment at 1950 ka (Shackleton et al., 1990; Hilgen, 1991a) within MIS 71 (Shackleton et al., 1990; Lourens et al., 1992, 1996a) or MIS 74 (Lisiecki and Raymo, 2005). In addition, three magnetic excursions are observed at Site U1308: Punaruu at 1092 ka in MIS 31/32 (Fig. 10), Gilsa at 1584 ka in MIS 54/55 (Fig. 11) and Porcupine at 2737 ka in MIS G6/G7 (Fig. 12). The Porcupine excursion is a newly-recognized excursion, and confirmation of this excursion requires its identification elsewhere.  

For the 1200e2200 ka interval, North Atlantic RPI Stacks 1e3, comprising 3, 4 and 5 RPI records, respectively, are compared with the Site U1308 RPI record in Fig. 13. All five RPI records used in the stacks (IODP Sites U1308 and U1307, ODP Sites 982, 983 and 984) are placed on their individual published age models with a single adjustment by linear interpolation (from the top of the Olduvai to the top of the Re union Subchron) to account for the revised age of the onset of the Olduvai Subchron. As dictated by the age ranges of the records themselves, the final RPI Stack 3 (NARPI-2200) comprises all 5 North Atlantic records for 1.2e1.9 Ma (although there is a data gap in the 1.45e1.55 Ma interval at Site U1307), only 4 records (excluding Site 983) for 1.9e2.1 Ma, and 3 records (excluding Sites 983 and 984) for 2.1e2.2 Ma.  

The NARPI-2200 stack (thick dark blue line in Figs. 13 and 14, with the 2s error in grey shading) shows correspondence with alternative stacks:PISO, Sint-2000, PADM2M and Epapis (Fig. 14). Prominent RPI minima are associated with the Cobb Mt. Subchron (\~1200 ka), the boundaries of the Olduvai Subchron (1780 ka and 1925 ka), and the R eunion Subchron (2150 ka) as well as the Gilsa excursion (1584 ka), and the Gardar excursion (1470 ka) recorded at ODP Sites 983 and 984 (Channell et al., 2002) but not at Site U1308. The correspondence between the NARPI-2200 stack, Epapis, Sint-2000 and PADM2M goes beyond the subchron boundaries, for example, some RPI minima within polarity chrons (e.g., \~1360 ka, \~1460 ka and \~1570 ka) appear to be present all three stacks (Fig. 14). Beyond 2.2 Ma, the Site U1308 RPI record can be compared with the IODP Site U1314 RPI record (Ohno et al., 2012) and with the Epapis stack. Prominent features in RPI records can be matched for Sites U1314 and U1308, and to a less extent with Epapis (Fig. 15).  

In the Sint-2000 and PADM2M stacks, that extend back to 2.0 Ma, there is evidence for lower mean geomagnetic field intensities in the Matuyama Chron than in the Brunhes Chron, by \~23% according to Ziegler et al. (2011). By scaling the Site U1308 RPI record to VADM (Fig. 16), using the same scaling method used for the PISO stack (Channell et al., 2009), we find that the Site U1308 mean VADM for the Matuyama Chron and for the Brunhes Chron (7.05   1022 Am2) are indistinguishable, implying that the geomagnetic field strength is not a function of polarity state. The apparent difference in RPI/VADM between the Brunhes and Matuyama Chrons in Sint-2000 and PADM2M may be attributed to viscous remanence (VRM) acquired in the Brunhes Chron that reduced the magnetization intensity of some Matuyama data included in the stacks.  


Comparison of the North Atlantic RPI stack with the Pacific Epapis stack in the 1.2e2.2 Ma interval (Fig. 14) appears to indicate that the North Atlantic stack lags the Pacific stack. This lag was pointed out in comparison of the PISO and Epapis stacks (Channell et al., 2009), and in comparison of the Site U1314 record and Epapis (Ohno et al., 2012), and we attribute it to the method used to generate the age models for cores included in the Epapis stack. To provide age models for cores included in Epapis, magnetic concentration parameters (ARM intensity and magnetic susceptibility) were correlated to the LR04 calibrated d18O template (Yamazaki and Oda, 2005). Glacial isotopic stages were correlated with magnetic concentration minima, implying biogenic carbonate dilution of magnetic concentration during glacial stages (the so-called Pacific carbonate pattern). The correlation may have induced a lag because magnetic concentration minima may be associated with late glacial intervals or Terminations, rather than glacial marine isotopic stages themselves.  

# 8. Conclusions  

IODP Site U1308 now has relatively high-resolution d18O and RPI records for the last 3.2 Ma, with 2e10 cm sample spacing for d18O, and nominally 1-cm spacing for RPI although adjacent RPI determinations are not independent due to the finite size of the magnetometer response function (Weeks et al.,1993; Guyodo et al., 2002). The mean sedimentation rate for the entire Site U1308 record is \~8.5 cm/kyr. A magnetic stratigraphy comprising polarity reversals, magnetic excursions (Punaruu, Gilsa and Porcupine), and relative paleointensity (RPI) has been calibrated using the Site U1308 d18O record through correlation to LR04. The calibrated templates for RPI and d18O from Site U1308 (central Atlantic), augmented by three magnetic excursions, can be used as a reference template for improvement of Late Pliocene and Quaternary stratigraphies. Magnetic excursions constitute brief, millennialscale, directional aberrations of the geomagnetic field that are only sporadically recorded at sedimentation rates similar to those at Site U1308, although they are believed to involve the demise and regeneration of the main axial dipole field that therefore are useful, when recorded, for global stratigraphic correlation (Channell, 2014).  

The North Atlantic RPI stack for 1.2e2.2 Ma (NARPI-2200), compiled from five records, including Site U1308, all placed on their individual age models, with modification for a revised age for the onset of the Olduvai Subchron (1925 ka), is compared with published stacks (Epapis, Sint-2000 and PADM2M) that cover all or part of the same interval. The resolution (mean sedimentation rate), and quality of the age models, of individual records controls the resolution of each stack, and other stacks are clearly smoothed relative to NARPI-2200 reflecting the lower sedimentation rates associated with RPI records from outside the Atlantic Ocean. An apparent lag of NARPI-2200 relative to the Pacific Epapis by a few tens of kiloyears, in some parts of the record, is associated with the procedure of age model construction in Epapis.  

The ages and MIS assignments of Matuyama and Gauss polarity reversals at Site U1308 are largely consistent with the current GPTS (Lourens et al., 2004; Ogg, 2012), apart from the base of the Olduvai  

Subchronozone that occurs in MIS 73 at 1925 ka, rather than 1950 ka. The ages for Quaternary polarity reversals in the current GPTS originate from correlation, through benthic d18O, of the astrochronology from ODP Site 677 to the magnetic stratigraphy of DSDP Site 607 (Shackleton et al., 1990). This methodology, however, was not used for the onset of the Olduvai Subchron, that was dated by association of the polarity reversal with the extinction of D. brouweri  and  with  MIS  71  (Backman  and  Shackleton, 1983; Ruddiman et al., 1986) at 1950 ka (Shackleton et al., 1990). The onset of the Olduvai was recorded in MIS 73 at DSDP Site 607 (Raymo et al., 1989), and this placement is confirmed at IODP Site U1308 leading to an age of 1925 ka based on the Site U1308 age model derived by correlation to the LR04 benthic d18O stack (Lisiecki and Raymo, 2005). The ages of Quaternary polarity reversals from Shackleton et al. (1990) have very largely stood the test of time, and have been corroborated by numerous subsequent studies. In the case of the onset of the Olduvai Subchron, however, the conventional association of the polarity reversal with MIS 71 and 1.95 Ma (Shackleton et al., 1990) should be revised to MIS 73 and 1925 ka.
